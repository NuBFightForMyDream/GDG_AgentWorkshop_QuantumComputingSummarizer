Observables and Operators
• Observables are something we can observe and measure.
• We say that if we measure a state, it will collapse to one of the basis states corresponding to an observable.
Then it means that the basis state (vector) must be a value we can understand classically.
• To measure the spin in the Ƹ𝑧 direction, we need to apply a magnetic field in the Ƹ𝑧 direction, and through
some processes (measurement, photon emission), we measure its energy.
• If it has lower energy, we know it collapses to the |↑⟩ state. If it has higher energy, we know it collapses to
the |↓⟩ state.
• For measurement the spin in the ො
𝑥 direction, if the energy level measured is low, we know it collapses to
the |←⟩ state. If the energy level measured is high, we know it collapses to the |→⟩ state.
• We see that every measurement corresponds to an observable and each measurement result must be a basis
state corresponding to that observable/measurement.
• For the position observable, the basis contains the basis states of (x, y, z), and since x, y, z can take any
values, it has an infinite number of basis states.
• In summary, a basis formed by the basis states corresponding to an observable is a convenient choice in
the formulation of the quantum computing problems.

Observables and Operators
• There are two formalisms in quantum mechanics. One is the wave quantum mechanics by Erwin
Schrödinger or Schrödinger Equation, which describes how a quantum state evolves.
• The other formalism is the matrix quantum mechanics. It was proposed independently by Werner
Heisenberg. It has exactly the same content as the wave quantum mechanics including Schrödinger
equation, but all in matrix form.
• The matrix quantum mechanics is very useful in quantum computing. It is particularly powerful when we
combine it with Dirac’s bra–ket notation.
• We represent quantum mechanical states as vectors. And we use an operator to describe the evolution of a
quantum state.
• An operator operates on a vector. It transforms the vector from one to another.
• The transformation can be stretching and rotation, etc.
• In quantum computing, it is nothing but just the rotation of vectors (states) in the hyperspace.
• In the matrix quantum mechanics, operators are represented as matrix.

Summary
• Operators as matrices: In quantum mechanics, operators are represented by matrices that determine
how a quantum state evolves (time evolution/transformations in Hilbert space).
• Rotation vs scaling: Matrices generally “rotate” states in Hilbert space, but their eigenvectors are
special—they are not rotated, only scaled.
• Eigenpairs: If 𝐴∣𝑎⟩=𝑎∣𝑎⟩, then ∣𝑎⟩ is an eigenvector of 𝐴 with eigenvalue 𝑎.
• Observable bases: For convenience, quantum computing typically uses the eigenvectors of
observables as the computational basis.
• Classical readout: The eigenvalues of observables correspond to the classically measurable outcomes.
• Why it matters: Knowing what eigenvectors/eigenvalues are—and how to find them—is essential for
analyzing measurements and state evolution.
• Action point: Practice computing eigenvalues/eigenvectors to build fluency for quantum problems.

• Any quantum mechanical state is a vector. We can decompose the state (i.e. the vector) into a linear
combination of some basis vectors that span the space of interest.
• The eigenvectors of the matrices corresponding to observables are commonly used as the basis states.
• This is because after all, we need to retrieve information from the quantum computer through
measurement and the states must collapse to one of the basis states, which is one of the eigenstates of the
matrices corresponding to the observables.

space because it is easier to implement quantum computers using two-level systems. That is the reason we
still use “bit” in the qubit, as bit stands for “binary digit” like in classical computing. It is possible to use
3-level systems for quantum computing too, but this is less practical. One of the common two-level
