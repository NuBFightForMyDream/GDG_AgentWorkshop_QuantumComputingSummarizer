Background - Encryption
• Nowadays, data encryption is an indispensable part of our daily life.
• In the encryption process, we usually generate a very big integer number, N, using two large prime numbers, P
and Q, such that 𝑁 = 𝑃 × 𝑄.
• N is shared with the public, and only the persons who have a key can find P and Q quickly.
• Otherwise, to break the encryption (e.g. in RSA), one needs to be able to perform prime integer factorization
of N, i.e. to find the values of P and Q.
• There is no known method to do it quickly. Internet security relies almost entirely on the fact/expectation that
no one can perform the prime integer factorization efficiently.

Introduction
• The HHL algorithm, proposed by Aram Harrow, Avinatan Hassidim, and Seth Lloyd in 2009, is used for
solving linear systems of equations using the principles of quantum computing.
• To solve such a system, we cast our problem in the form A|xۧ = |bۧ, where |xۧ and |bۧ are normalized vectors
and A is a hermitian matrix.
• The process involves finding the eigenvalues of the matrix by making use of the Quantum Phase Estimation
(QPE) sub-routine. This in turn makes use of the inverse Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT).
• The determined eigenvalues are then used to implement a controlled rotation to effectively find the inverse
of the matrix A. This allows us to calculate |xۧ = 𝐴−1|bۧ. The final step is to uncompute the phase estimation.

Time and Resource Efficiency
• Classical Runtimes: Standard classical methods like Gaussian elimination take 𝒪(𝑁3) time. However, this
drops to 𝒪(𝑁 𝑘) if the matrix is sparse and well-conditioned, and you only need a summary statistic.
• HHL Algorithm Runtime: This quantum algorithm achieves an exponentially faster runtime of 𝒪(𝐾2 log 𝑁
𝜖 ),
assuming the matrix is 𝑠-sparse (maximum of 𝑠 non-zero elements per row) and 𝜖 is the acceptable margin
of error.
• Condition Number (𝒌): The ratio of the smallest to largest eigenvalue, which indicates the system's
sensitivity to perturbations.
• Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE) Challenges: QPE is a major source of error and is highly resource-
intensive. It requires many qubits and gates, scaling with the system size, and its computational complexity
scales as 𝒪(1/𝜖) based on the desired accuracy of the eigenvalues.
• Eigenvalue Inversion Challenges: This stage is also resource-heavy because it relies on a non-unitary
transformation, which may require multiple attempts to successfully produce the required ȁ ۧ1 state.
